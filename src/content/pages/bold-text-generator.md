## How to Make Text Bold or Italic in Four Steps

The whole thing takes about ten seconds. Everything updates as you type, so you can line up all six styles and compare them before you copy anything. The step worth slowing down for is the last one, because bold text can look perfect in the box you typed it into and fall apart in the place you posted it.

### Step 1. Type your whole phrase

Type the full thing you plan to post, not a test word. Include any numbers. Italic has no digits at all, so a year, a price or a phone number stays plain while the letters around it change. You want to see that before it goes somewhere public.

### Step 2. Pick one of the six styles

Six styles, two decisions. Serif or sans serif first, then bold, italic or both. Sans serif usually looks better inside a social app, because it sits closer to the font the app already uses and the text reads as deliberate instead of pasted in. Serif carries more weight, which suits a single word doing a job, like a name or a label.

### Step 3. Copy it

One tap copies the whole line. On a phone, use the copy button instead of selecting the text by hand, because a long press usually grabs the space at the end and a few fields reject anything with a trailing space. Small thing. It causes a surprising number of failed pastes.

### Step 4. Check it where it is going

Paste it into the real field and look at it before you publish. A composer preview and a published post don't always render the same, and a desktop browser proves nothing about what a phone will do. Fair warning: this is also the step where some people discover the app had a bold button the whole time.

## Every Bold and Italic Style There Actually Is (There Are Six)

Here is the entire set. Not a selection, not the popular ones. Six alphabets, which is every bold and italic variant Unicode has ever encoded. The same phrase runs through each one so you can see what changes and what stays put.

| Style | Serif | Sans serif |
|---|---|---|
| Bold | 𝐖𝐚𝐭𝐜𝐡 𝐓𝐡𝐢𝐬 𝟐𝟒 | 𝗪𝗮𝘁𝗰𝗵 𝗧𝗵𝗶𝘀 𝟮𝟰 |
| Italic | 𝑊𝑎𝑡𝑐ℎ 𝑇ℎ𝑖𝑠 24 | 𝘞𝘢𝘵𝘤𝘩 𝘛𝘩𝘪𝘴 24 |
| Bold italic | 𝑾𝒂𝒕𝒄𝒉 𝑻𝒉𝒊𝒔 24 | 𝙒𝙖𝙩𝙘𝙝 𝙏𝙝𝙞𝙨 24 |

Two decisions produce all six. Serif or sans serif, then bold, italic or both. Look at the digits while you're there, because half the grid has them and half doesn't. That single detail is the thing most other pages get wrong.

### The one letter that had to be borrowed

Serif italic is missing a lowercase h. The slot where it belongs, U+1D455, was left empty when the block was encoded, so every generator including this one substitutes ℎ, a character officially named PLANCK CONSTANT that physics claimed first. It matches closely enough that almost nobody notices. The other five styles have no gaps at all, which is unusual for Unicode. If you want joined-up letterforms rather than a slant, the [cursive font generator](/cursive-font-generator/) handles those separately.

### Double-struck is called bold, and it isn't bold

You'll see 𝔹𝕠𝕝𝕕 listed as a bold option on other sites. It isn't one. The proper name is blackboard bold, and it was built so mathematicians could write number sets on a chalkboard: ℝ for real numbers, ℕ for natural numbers, ℤ for integers. Look closely and the strokes are outlined rather than thickened, which is why a word in it reads lighter than plain text instead of heavier.

### Alternating letters are a trick, not an alphabet

𝐚𝑙𝐭𝑒𝐫𝑛𝐚𝑡𝐢𝑛𝐠 looks like a seventh style. It isn't. It's two of the six above applied to every other letter, which any generator can produce by flipping between them as it goes. Some sites count three or four of these patterns as separate styles to inflate their totals. Be careful where you use it, because that pattern has been shorthand for sarcasm online for years, and plenty of readers will hear your sentence in a mocking voice.

### Bold versions of other alphabets

Two more bold styles exist, and they live on other pages because they're different alphabets rather than different weights. 𝓑𝓸𝓵𝓭 is bold script, which belongs with the joined-up styles. 𝕭𝖔𝖑𝖉 is bold blackletter, and the gothic font generator covers where it came from and where it breaks.

## Bold Text Is Not Bold Formatting. Here Is What It Actually Is

Press Ctrl+B and the letter stays a letter. The app just draws it heavier. Copy a bold word from this page and you get something else entirely, a different character that happens to be shaped like a heavy B. That difference explains everything else on this page.

|  | Real bold formatting | Pasted bold characters |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | An instruction to the app | A different character |
| Who applies it | The app you're typing in | You, before pasting |
| Survives copy and paste | Usually not | Yes |
| Works with no formatting button | No | Yes |
| Guaranteed to display | Yes | Depends on the reader's device |
| Findable by search | Yes | No |

These characters exist because of maths. In an equation a bold V and an italic v mean two different things, a vector and a scalar, and that difference has to survive when the equation gets written in plain text with no formatting available. Unicode encoded a full set of styled letters so the meaning would travel. Nobody added them so a bio could look louder.

That origin explains the odd parts. It's why the letters carry official names like MATHEMATICAL BOLD CAPITAL A. It's why bold has digits and italic doesn't, since equations needed bold vectors and never needed slanted numbers. And it's why the set stops at six, because six covered what the notation required.

## Bold or Italic: Which One Your Sentence Actually Needs

Bold stops the eye. It's for the one thing a person skimming must not miss: a deadline, a price, a warning, the name of the thing you're selling. It works by contrast, so it only works while most of the text around it stays plain. Bold everything and you've bolded nothing.

Italic changes how a sentence sounds in your head. English uses it for titles of books and films, for words borrowed from other languages, and for a word the writer wants you to lean on slightly. It's quieter than bold and it holds up over longer stretches, which is why a whole italic sentence still reads as a sentence while a whole bold sentence reads as shouting.

Bold italic is for the rare line that needs both jobs at once. Used once, it lands. Used four times in a bio, it reads as someone who couldn't decide.

One rule covers most cases. Pick a style, keep it for the whole profile or post, and change it only when the job changes. A bio running bold, italic and bold italic across three lines looks broken rather than designed. Quick note: none of the italic styles carry numbers, so if your line leads with a date or a price, bold is the only branch that styles the whole thing.

## Bold Text on LinkedIn, and Why It Is Riskier Than It Looks

LinkedIn is where this technique gets used most and questioned hardest. The platform has no formatting anywhere: not in a post, not in a headline, not in the about section. So the workaround is genuine, and a large share of the bold text you see there was pasted from a page like this one.

The problem is search. A company that sells a LinkedIn formatting tool publishes the warning itself, that LinkedIn search often ignores these characters, so a keyword styled in bold may not be indexed at all. Think about what that means for a headline. A recruiter searching for a job title will not find the person whose job title is bolded.

There's a second problem specific to this platform. LinkedIn carries a large concentration of accessibility professionals, and several of them write about this regularly. Comments pointing out that a formatted post is unreadable to a screen reader turn up under exactly the kind of post that uses it most.

A workable compromise exists. Style a divider, a section label or your own name, and leave every keyword in plain text: your job title, your skills, your company, the thing you want to be found for. Reality check: if bolding a word could cost you the search result, that word is too important to bold.

## Why Bold Text Eats Your Character Limit

Every styled character costs you two. Plain letters live in the first block of Unicode and count as one. These live thousands of positions further out, and platforms that weight characters by position count each one double.

The arithmetic is unkind. An Instagram bio holds 150 characters, so a fully styled bio holds around 75 letters. A 280 character post drops to roughly 140. Bios take the worst of it, because they're short to begin with and people tend to style the whole thing.

The cost is identical across all six styles. Switching from bold to italic saves nothing, and neither does choosing sans serif over serif. The only thing that buys back room is styling less of the text. Our small text page hits the same rule from the opposite direction, where people assume smaller letters take up less space and they take up more.

## Why Some Bold Letters Show as Boxes, and Which Ones Never Do

Bold is the safest thing on this page. Serif and sans serif bold have been in system fonts for years and effectively never fail on a current device. Italic and bold italic carry the risk, especially sans serif italic, which is the one older handsets are most likely to be missing.

When a device doesn't have a character, it does one of two things. It borrows the shape from a different font, which is why one word can come out taller or thinner than the rest of your sentence. Or it draws an empty rectangle, the failure everyone recognises. The second one is honest. The first one is worse, because your post looks slightly off and you never find out why.

None of this is the tool or your phone. It's whichever fonts are installed on the device of the person reading, which is outside everyone's control including the platform's. Heads-up: the only reliable test is your own phone plus one older one, which takes two minutes and settles the question for good.

## Bold Has Numbers. Italic Does Not.

Bold has a full set of digits. Two sets, in fact: 𝟎𝟏𝟐𝟑𝟒𝟓𝟔𝟕𝟖𝟗 in serif and 𝟬𝟭𝟮𝟯𝟰𝟱𝟲𝟳𝟴𝟵 in sans serif. Italic has none. Not italic, not bold italic, not the sans serif version of either.

That's why the test phrase on this page ends in a number. 𝑾𝒂𝒕𝒄𝒉 𝑻𝒉𝒊𝒔 24 shows exactly what happens: the letters change and the digits sit there in plain text, at a different weight from everything around them. In a bio it reads as a mistake rather than a style.

The reason goes back to the equations. Mathematical notation needed bold digits for vectors and matrices, so they were encoded. Italic was already doing a different job in maths, where a slanted letter means a variable, and a slanted 2 would have meant nothing useful to anyone. Nobody asked for them, so nobody made them.

Two ways around it. Use bold for any line containing a date, a price or a phone number, since bold is the only branch of the six with digits. Or leave the numbers plain deliberately and style only the words, which looks intentional as long as you do it consistently.

## What a Screen Reader Hears When You Bold a Whole Paragraph

This is the section most font sites skip, and it's the one that should change how you use the tool. Screen reading software doesn't see a bold word. It sees a run of mathematical symbols shaped like letters, and it has to decide what to do with them.

There are two outcomes and both are bad. In the first, the software reads the character names out one at a time, so a single bolded word turns into a recital of MATHEMATICAL BOLD CAPITAL W, MATHEMATICAL BOLD SMALL A, and onward. In the second, it skips the styled run completely and says nothing. The second one is worth sitting with. The word you chose to emphasise is the word that disappears.

Which behaviour you get depends on the software and its settings. NVDA, JAWS and VoiceOver all handle these characters differently, so the same post can be a recital on one setup and silence on another. An article published in August 2023 by an accessibility specialist described the practice as damaging to accessibility, findability and predictability at the same time, and working accessibility professionals have repeated the point ever since. In one widely read public thread, a developer explained the character-name behaviour and the site's owner, who has a vision impairment, confirmed it firsthand.

Bold carries more of this risk than any other style. Nobody writes a whole bio in blackletter, but people bold entire paragraphs, whole posts and every heading in an update. The longer the styled run, the more there is to lose.

Three rules keep the look without the damage. Keep anything that must be read in plain text, and style only what's decoration. Never bold a full paragraph or a full post. And if a line carries information someone might actually need, like a date, a price, an address or a link, leave it alone completely.

## Why Search Stops Finding Your Bolded Words

A search box compares characters, not shapes. When someone searches for a word, the system looks for those exact letters in that exact order. A styled version contains none of them, so the match never happens, however identical the two look on screen.

You can prove it in ten seconds. Paste a styled word into the search box on any site and watch it return nothing, then paste the plain version and watch the results appear. The same thing happens to hashtags, which stop working as hashtags the moment the letters change.

The places this costs you are predictable. A job title in a headline, a product name in a bio, a skill on a profile, your own name if you styled it. Those are the words you most want to be found for, and they're also the words people style most often, because they're the ones that feel important.

So the rule writes itself. Style the words that decorate. Leave the words that get searched.

## How Much of Your Text to Bold

Bold works by contrast, so the amount matters more than the style. A commonly repeated guideline says to bold about a tenth of your text. That number isn't measured from anything, but the instinct behind it holds: emphasis stops working the moment there's a lot of it.

Three patterns that hold up:

- Bold the first line of a bio and leave the rest plain. The eye lands there anyway.
- One label per paragraph. A short bolded lead-in, then plain text for the explanation.
- A single call to action at the end, where you want the tap.

The failure state is easy to picture. A fully bold post reads as shouting, a fully bold bio reads as broken on the phones that render it unevenly, and a post using three styles at once reads like a ransom note. One catch worth naming: bold renders heavier on some phones than others, so a line that looks balanced on your screen can look aggressive on someone else's.

## Bold in Word, Google Docs and Canva: Just Press Ctrl+B

If you're in a document, close this page and press Ctrl+B. Cmd+B on a Mac. Word, Google Docs, Canva, Pages, Slides and every other editor has real bold built in, and the shortcut has worked the same way for decades.

Real bold does three things pasted characters can't. It changes when you change the font, so the document stays consistent. It searches correctly, so find-and-replace still catches the word. And it exports cleanly, while pasted characters depend on a font being available at export or print time that might not be.

Pasting styled characters into a document is the one case where the result is worse in every direction. It won't match the document font, it breaks search inside the file, and it can reach the printer as a row of rectangles. Save the six styles for the places that give you no choice.
