You scroll past a Facebook post with bold text in it and wonder how that person did it. Then you go looking for the button, and there is no button. There are two different answers to that question, and they apply in completely different places on Facebook. One is a real formatting control that Facebook keeps tucked away in a single spot. The other is a copy-and-paste method that works almost everywhere else, and it comes with a set of rules that no font site bothers to mention.

## 1. How to Change Your Facebook Font in Four Steps

### Step 1. Type your text

Type the full phrase you plan to post, not a single test word. Some styles are missing letters, and those gaps only appear once you can see a whole sentence. Every style updates as you type, so you can compare them side by side before you commit to one.

### Step 2. Pick a style

Styles fall into three groups: the ones that render everywhere, the ones that render almost everywhere, and the ones that leave gaps on older phones. If your post is going to a wide audience, stay in the first group. Save the riskier styles for short bursts where a missing letter will not ruin the sentence.

### Step 3. Copy it

Press copy on the style you want. Copying and pasting on Facebook behaves the same on both platforms: tap and hold to paste on a phone, or use the usual keyboard shortcut on a computer. What lands on your clipboard is plain text as far as your phone is concerned, which is exactly why it travels into fields that have no formatting buttons at all. The same string behaves the same way in a post, a comment or a message.

### Step 4. Paste and check

Paste it where you actually plan to publish, then look at the result before you hit post. The composer preview and the live post do not always agree, and a Page post and a personal post can render differently on the same phone. Checking takes five seconds and saves you from a post full of empty rectangles.

<h2 id="bold">2. Facebook Does Have Bold and Italic. It Is Just Hidden in One Place</h2>

Most people working out how to bold text on Facebook assume the feature does not exist, and go hunting for a Facebook bold text generator instead. It does exist, and it gives you genuine formatting, the same kind you would get in a word processor. The catch is where Facebook put it.

The formatting toolbar lives inside Facebook Group posts, and only when you are writing from a computer. Start a post in a group, highlight some text, and a small bubble appears with a B and an I in it. The same bubble gives you headers, bulleted lists, numbered lists and block quotations. Facebook's own help page states the limit plainly: you can only format a group post on a computer.

That one sentence explains almost every confused thread on the subject. Someone tries the same trick on their timeline and nothing happens. Someone else tries it in the mobile app and finds no bubble at all. The control simply is not there outside group posts on desktop.

So here is the full picture for bold text in a Facebook post. Real formatting: group posts, desktop browser. No real formatting: timeline posts, Page posts, comments, your bio, Marketplace listings, Messenger, and the entire mobile app on both iOS and Android.

Worth knowing: the two methods are not equivalent, even when the result looks the same. Text bolded with the B button is still the letter B underneath, so search can find it and a screen reader reads it normally. Pasted styled text swaps in different characters that only look like letters, which is why it works in places the button cannot reach and also why it causes the problems covered further down this page.

<h2 id="names">5. Your Name, Your Username and Your Page Name Follow Three Different Rules</h2>

A lot of sites lump these together and tell you to style your name. They are three separate systems with three separate rule sets. Getting them mixed up can leave you stuck with a name you did not choose for the next two months.

### Your display name

This is the name that appears above your posts. Facebook's help page on the subject is direct about what is not allowed: no unusual capitalisation, no punctuation, no unusual characters, no random words. The banned list also covers numbers, repeating characters, characters from more than one language, professional or religious titles, and words used in place of a name.

Then there is the part that catches people out. Once you change your name, you cannot change it again for sixty days. Fair warning: if you push a styled name through and Facebook later reverts it or you decide you hate it, that sixty-day clock may already be running. TikTok runs a far looser version of the same rule, and our TikTok fonts guide puts a nickname with no waiting period next to a handle locked for thirty days.

### Your username

Your username is the part after facebook.com in your profile link. Facebook restricts it to letters, digits and full stops, with a minimum of five characters. Full stops and capitalisation do not create separate usernames either, so johnsmith55 and John.Smith55 are treated as the same handle. That character set leaves no room at all for styled letters, which is why every generator on the internet fails here.

### Your Page name

Business Pages get reviewed against their own naming guidelines. Those rules bar improper capitalisation such as tHe best CaFE, symbols like the registered trademark mark, unnecessary punctuation, and slogans or descriptions tacked onto the end. A styled Page name runs straight into the capitalisation and symbol rules, and a Page name rejection is a slower problem to fix than a personal one.

<h2 id="ads">6. Ads, Boosted Posts and Marketplace Strip or Reject Your Styling</h2>

This is the section that matters most if you spend money on Facebook, and it is the one every font site skips. Meta's advertising standards list Unicode characters used to obfuscate words or phrases as something you are not allowed to do in ad text. That wording is broad, and the people who decide whether your styling counts as obfuscation are not you.

The reason behind the rule is mechanical. Facebook's ad review system reads your copy to check it against policy. Styled characters are not the letters they resemble, so an automated scan cannot read them as words. Text a review system cannot read looks a great deal like text someone is hiding on purpose.

One catch makes this worse than a simple rejection. Repeated policy violations can restrict your advertising privileges or close the ad account entirely, and arguing that the fancy font was innocent is a slow and uncertain process. Boosting an existing post carries the same risk, because a boosted post passes through the same review as a fresh ad.

Marketplace is a different problem with the same outcome. Listing titles feed Facebook's internal search, and a buyer typing sofa will not match a title spelled with mathematical alphanumeric characters. Some sellers also report listings rejected for invalid characters in the title.

The safe approach is straightforward. Keep ad copy, headlines, Page names and Marketplace titles in plain text. Save styled characters for organic posts, comments and your own bio, where nothing is scanning them for compliance.

## 7. Facebook Fonts Are Not Fonts. Here Is What They Actually Are

Sites sell these characters under a dozen different labels. A Facebook font changer, an FB font generator, a fancy text generator, a lettertype converter and a Facebook fonts online tool are all handing you the same substituted characters. The name on the site changes nothing about what lands on your clipboard.

When you copy a styled phrase from any generator, you are not copying a font. Fonts are files that live on your device and control how existing letters are drawn. What you copied is a set of entirely different characters that happen to be shaped like letters.

Most of them come from a Unicode block called Mathematical Alphanumeric Symbols, added so that mathematicians could write a bold vector and an italic variable as distinct things in plain text. A bold S from that block is a separate character with its own code point and its own formal name. As far as any computer is concerned, it has no relationship to the letter S at all.

Straight answer on why this matters: the distinction explains every quirk on this page at once. Search cannot match characters that are only shaped like the letters you typed. Neither can an ad review system, a screen reader, or the validator sitting behind a username field.

| | Real formatting (the B button) | Pasted styled text |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What changes | How the letter is drawn | The letter itself |
| Searchable | Yes | No |
| Screen reader | Reads normally | Reads badly or not at all |
| Allowed in ads | Yes | Risky |
| Works in name fields | Not applicable | Rejected |
| Works in the mobile app | No | Yes |

The last two rows hold the whole trade-off in miniature. Substituted characters reach places real formatting cannot, including the mobile app and every field without a toolbar. They also break the things real formatting keeps intact, and that is the price you pay for the reach.

## 8. What Font Does Facebook Actually Use?

Facebook does not ship one font to everyone. The interface uses a system font stack, which means it asks your device for whatever font that device already uses and falls back through a list until something matches. On Windows you get Segoe UI, on a Mac or iPhone you get San Francisco, and on Android you get Roboto.

This sits behind something people notice and misdiagnose. The same post genuinely looks different on your phone and your friend's laptop, and neither of you did anything wrong. It also means there is no Facebook site font to download or install, because the letters you see already belong to your own operating system.

### The logo font is not something you can copy

The wordmark is a different story. Facebook's current logo uses Facebook Sans, a proprietary typeface built for the company by the type foundry Dalton Maag. The original 2005 wordmark used a modified version of Klavika Bold, a typeface by Eric Olson, with the customisations handled by the agency Cuban Council.

Good to know: several sites describe Facebook Sans as a custom version of Helvetica, which is not correct. It is an original design, and like the Klavika modifications before it, it is not licensed for public use. Nothing in any font generator will reproduce it.

## 9. Why Some Letters Show as Boxes, and Which Ones Never Do

A box, or an empty rectangle, means the device displaying your post has no glyph for that character. The character arrived intact. Whoever is reading simply has no font on their phone that knows how to draw it. Typographers call that box a tofu, which is where the Noto font family got its name, short for no tofu.

### Tier one: renders everywhere

Bold, Bold Sans, Handwriting and monospace sit in the Mathematical Alphanumeric Symbols block. This block has been in Unicode since 2001 and font support for it is close to universal. If your post uses only these, boxes are very unlikely.

### Tier two: exists, but the font might be missing it

Classic Script, double-struck, Small Caps, Fullwidth and Circled all exist as proper characters, but they live in blocks that some fonts cover only partly. Older Android builds and cheaper devices are where you see the failures. This is the tier where one letter breaks and the rest of the word survives.

### Tier three: does not exist at all

A few letters were never encoded in some of these sets. When a generator hits one of those, it leaves the plain letter in place rather than substituting a lookalike. That is why a Small Caps phrase can contain one full-size letter, and it is a deliberate choice rather than a bug.

## 10. Does Styled Text Hurt Your Reach? Feed and Search Are Different

The honest answer needs two halves. Facebook runs two separate systems over your text, and they treat styled characters very differently. Most advice online collapses them into a single question and then gets the answer wrong in both directions.

Feed ranking works mainly on behaviour. Whether people stop, react, comment and share carries the weight, and there is no evidence that Facebook demotes a post for containing Unicode characters. If a styled hook makes people slow down, that is a real effect, and if it makes them scroll past because it looks like spam, that is a real effect too.

Search is the half people forget. Facebook search matches what you typed against what is stored, and a styled character is not the letter it resembles. A Page named with script characters will not surface when someone types the plain name. The same applies to Marketplace titles, group names and anything else people find by typing.

You will see specific numbers quoted around the internet, claims like forty per cent more engagement or a doubling of join requests. None of them cite a study, and none of them survive a look at the source. Treat styled text as a design choice with a discoverability cost rather than a growth tactic.

## 11. What a Screen Reader Hears When You Style a Post

Screen readers read the underlying characters, and the shape on screen means nothing to them. When one hits a run of mathematical alphanumeric characters, it does one of three things. It reads out the formal Unicode name of every character in turn, it spells the phrase letter by letter in a flat voice, or it skips the run entirely and stays silent.

Reality check on what that sounds like. A styled bio of 101 characters can turn into a minute of a machine reciting mathematical bold capital S, mathematical bold small u, and onward. Nobody listens through that. They move on, and your bio has effectively said nothing.

The fix costs almost nothing. Keep your name, your bio and the substance of your post in plain characters, and use styling for one short line where losing the words does no damage. On desktop group posts, the B button gives you emphasis with none of this cost, because the letters underneath stay ordinary letters.

## 12. How Much of Your Post to Style

Style the hook and leave the rest alone. Facebook hides everything past roughly 125 characters on mobile behind See more, so the opening line is the part doing the work. One styled sentence at the top earns attention; a fully styled post reads as noise and gets scrolled.

Facebook bios are tighter still. At 101 characters you have room for about fifteen words, and every one of them is doing a job. Styling all of it costs you searchability and screen reader access for the whole field, so pick a single word if you want the effect at all.

Comments deserve a mention as well. A styled comment stands out in a thread of plain replies, which is useful once and irritating by the fourth time. Use it when the comment matters, not as a signature.
