Your letters don't get replaced here. Glitch text works by stacking combining marks onto the letters you already typed, which is why the effect can be turned up, turned down, and taken back off again. That makes it behave differently from every other generator on this site. Heavier settings can slow an app down, some platforms delete it the moment you post, and a few cut the effect off at a fixed number of marks. This page has ten settings, and it says where each one survives.

<h2 id="where">Where Glitch Text Works, and Where It Gets Deleted</h2>

Rendering and permission are two different questions. Almost every modern app can draw these marks, and plenty of them will still remove your message for using them. The sections below cover both, and where a community publishes an actual rule about it, you'll find the wording here.

### Discord

Discord renders it perfectly and then, on a lot of servers, deletes it. AutoMod rules can match these characters by pattern, and ready-made rule sets for exactly this circulate publicly, so a server doesn't need anyone technical to switch it on. Plenty of servers ban it in their written rules as well. Usernames also get forced to lowercase, which changes your output before anyone else sees it.

There's one fix worth keeping. If a heavy paste freezes the message box so you can't send it or clear it, select all and press backspace. It empties in one go where deleting by hand does nothing.

### Minecraft

Minecraft has its own glitch effect built in, and it isn't this one. A formatting code makes characters cycle constantly on screen, which is a genuinely different thing from stacked marks. It also works in places pasted text doesn't, so it's usually the better answer inside the game.

### Roblox

Sources disagree here, so this section lays out both claims and leaves them open. One Roblox-focused reference says usernames accept letters, numbers and a single underscore, that display names follow the same rule despite what generator sites claim, and that unrecognised characters get replaced with hash symbols. One thing isn't in dispute. Deliberately using characters to get past a text filter breaches Roblox's terms, whatever the field happens to accept. That applies to every platform on this page.

### Instagram and TikTok

Both render the marks in bios and captions. The character cost is the real limit here, because an Instagram bio holds 150 characters and one heavily glitched word can eat most of them. Search won't match the styled version either, so keep anything you want found in plain text. Our [Instagram font guide](/instagram-fonts/) takes the fields one at a time.

### Steam and gaming profiles

Steam profiles and most game clients render it without complaint, which makes them one of the friendlier homes for the heavier settings. Name fields are usually short, so the count matters more than the rendering does. Some reporting and moderation systems flag unusual character use in names, though nothing published confirms glitch text specifically.

### Forums, wikis and comment sections

This is where it gets banned most consistently. Wiki and forum rules commonly prohibit it outright, and the reason is practical: heavy marks overflow into other people's posts and make a thread hard to read. The clearest example is the wiki dedicated to the horror character this effect is named after. Its rules say "Do NOT post Zalgo text", and warn that doing so will subject you to a ban. If the community built around the lore won't allow it, most general forums won't either.

<h2 id="what-it-is">What Glitch Text Actually Is, and Why It's Not a Font</h2>

Nothing here changes your letters. A glitch generator takes the word you typed and appends combining marks after each character, and the app you paste into draws those marks on top of, under or through the letter in front of them. Your G is still a G underneath. That one fact explains the removers, the search problem and the character cost all at once.

### Glitch, zalgo, cursed, creepy, corrupted

Five words, one mechanism. They describe how heavy the effect is and what it's being used for. That's why each one appears here as a setting.

| Word | What people usually mean | A different mechanism? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glitch text | The general term, any intensity | No |
| Zalgo | The dense stacked look, named after a 2004 internet horror character | No |
| Cursed text | The heaviest end, often used as a joke | No |
| Creepy text | Thin marks spread wide, horror styling | No |
| Corrupted text | Dense and low, the broken-display look | No |

Sites running a separate page for each of these words are running the same generator behind all of them. The output is identical and so is the code underneath. The words are marketing rather than categories.

### How the marks stack

Combining marks are an ordinary part of Unicode and they exist for real languages. An accent over an e, a cedilla under a c, and the marks that Vietnamese, Arabic and dozens of other writing systems depend on. Each one is its own character that attaches to whatever came before it. Glitch text does nothing clever. It just attaches far more of them than any language ever would.

### Where the name came from

Zalgo started in July 2004 on the Something Awful forums. Somebody began editing newspaper comic strips, Nancy and Archie first and Garfield soon after, replacing the dialogue with warnings about an approaching entity. The entity picked up titles along the way, including He Who Waits Behind the Wall, and a signature line: He comes. The stacked, overflowing text arrived as its visual signature and outlived the comics by two decades.

### The thirty-mark ceiling

There's a published limit, and it's the reason apps cut the effect off. Unicode's normalisation annex defines a Stream-Safe Text Format, which caps the marks following a single base character at thirty. The specification says plainly that thirty sits far beyond anything any language or technical use actually requires, and a separate standards paper puts the longest genuinely meaningful run at seventeen. So when a platform clips your text, it isn't being arbitrary. It's applying a rule that has a name and a number. Here is the ceiling: thirty marks per letter, after which software is entitled to stop drawing. Anything generated above that line is decoration you're hoping gets rendered rather than text you can count on.

None of this is formatting. Pressing a bold button tells an app to draw a letter differently. Every generator on this site hands you different characters. Our [bold text generator](/bold-text-generator/) takes the swapping approach and this page takes the stacking approach, and both produce text that travels anywhere and obeys nobody.

<h2 id="limits">Does Glitch Text Work Everywhere? No, and Here Is What Breaks</h2>

No, and the pages promising universal compatibility are the ones to be most careful with. Three separate things can go wrong. The marks can render differently, they can get clipped, or they can genuinely slow software down. Which one you run into depends on the setting you picked and the app you're pasting into.

### Which settings are actually safe

Light and the direction-only settings survive nearly everywhere. Medium is fine in most places and blocked in some. Heavy and maximum are where real problems start, and they're best kept to a single word of decoration. The pattern is short enough to remember: mark count is risk.

### Why it looks different on someone else's screen

Your device draws these marks with whatever font it has installed. Two typefaces in common testing, Bitstream Vera Sans and DejaVu Sans, stack the marks into a neat vertical column, while most other fonts overlap them into a blur. So the same string can look carefully layered on your screen and like a smudge on someone else's. The text itself is identical in both cases. Only the font drawing it changed.

### When it lags or freezes

This is the part almost every other page leaves out, and all of it sits in public bug trackers. A code editor issue reports that pasting this text hangs the terminal, reproduced by the developers themselves. A chat client issue describes it causing font rendering to implode. A children's programming site warns its users away from it on low-powered machines.

Where this fails, it fails expensively. In January 2022 the author of a widely used programming package deliberately added an infinite loop that printed this text forever, breaking installs across millions of projects overnight. Wikipedia's entry on combining characters notes plainly that the technique can be used to halt or impair certain computer functions. That's no reason to avoid the light settings, and it is a reason not to paste a heavy one into somebody's terminal.

### Text that isn't English

Other writing systems take this badly. Languages that already use combining marks of their own, and scripts where letters join or reshape, end up with the added marks landing in the wrong place or breaking the joins entirely. A few generators advertise that they preserve other scripts, which tells you what the default behaviour is. Test a short string first if your text isn't in English.

### Quick fixes when something breaks

Three problems come up over and over, and each has a different answer. Two you can fix from your side and one you can't. Work through them in order before assuming the tool is at fault.

- Empty rectangles instead of marks. The reading device is missing that character. Drop to a lighter setting.
- The effect looks sliced off at the top or bottom. The app is clipping the line height, and there's nothing to change from your side.
- The message box freezes and won't clear. Select all, then press backspace.

The middle one is worth understanding, because it's the most common and the least fixable. Line height belongs to the app, so a bio field with a fixed row height will cut the top off your marks no matter what you generate. Our [small text generator](/small-text-generator/) meets the same counting problem from the other direction, where people expect smaller letters to cost less space and they cost more.

<h2 id="remove">How to Remove Glitch Text and Get Plain Words Back</h2>

The marks sit on top of your letters, so the original text is still there and comes back exactly. Every remover works the same way, stripping out the combining marks and leaving everything else alone. This tool does it too, so you can paste glitched text in and get clean words out.

### Which marks have to go

Five Unicode blocks hold these characters, and a complete remover has to clear all five. Most people never need the ranges themselves, but they matter if you're choosing between cleaning tools. A remover handling only the first block will look like it worked and quietly leave marks behind. Here is what a complete job covers.

| Block | Range |
| --- | --- |
| Combining Diacritical Marks | U+0300 to U+036F |
| Combining Diacritical Marks Extended | U+1AB0 to U+1AFF |
| Combining Diacritical Marks Supplement | U+1DC0 to U+1DFF |
| Combining Diacritical Marks for Symbols | U+20D0 to U+20FF |
| Combining Half Marks | U+FE20 to U+FE2F |

### Why clearing one block isn't enough

Most removers clear only the first row. That covers the marks European languages use, which is where the common ones live, but generators pull from all five blocks to get variety. A tool clearing one range leaves marks behind and looks like it half worked. <h2 id="readable">Keeping It Readable, and Who It Shuts Out</h2>

Two different questions sit inside readability. One is whether a sighted person can still make out the word, which the intensity settings handle. The other is whether the text works at all for somebody using assistive software, and that one has an uncomfortable answer.

### Keep it to a few words

A username, a bio line, one word inside a message. That's the range where this style does its job properly. Past that it stops being emphasis and turns into texture, and the reader either skips it or gives up. The one-word habit also happens to keep you under every platform's clipping threshold.

### What screen readers do with it

Screen reading software has no good option here. A public accessibility discussion describes it stuttering through the marks, reading nonsense, and severely hindering navigation for the people who depend on it. Two of the more honest generator sites say the same thing. One well-known generator page claims screen readers announce the underlying letters without trouble, which contradicts the reports coming from people actually using them. This page has not run its own NVDA or VoiceOver pass.

Until that test happens, this page assumes the worse outcome. Keep anything a person genuinely needs in plain text, and treat glitch text as decoration that some readers will never receive. Our [aesthetic font generator](/aesthetic-font-generator/) shares this caveat for the same reason, since both rely on characters assistive software was never built to narrate.

### Search won't find it

A search box matches characters in order. The marks sit between your letters in the underlying data, so a search for your word never lines up with the styled version even though every letter is still present. This is the detail that's easy to get half right. The base letters do survive, which is exactly why removers work, but they aren't next to each other any more, so the match fails.

That makes the rule straightforward. Anything you want found stays plain, meaning your name, your handle, the thing you do and any hashtag. Decoration can be as broken as you like.

### Don't use it to get past word filters

This gets recommended online and it's a poor idea in every direction. Platform terms treat deliberate filter evasion as a violation regardless of the method, so the outcome is the same whether the trick works or not. Moderation tools already decode it, and at least one remover is marketed directly at moderators for that job. Before you post it, be clear about which side of that line you're standing on.
