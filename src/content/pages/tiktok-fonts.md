TikTok gives you up to 160 characters for your bio. Some accounts still show the older limit of 80, so check your own field before you plan around it. That is not much room, and styled letters do not all take up the same amount of space. Some styles let you use the full 160. Others eat half of it before you finish your first line.

Every field on TikTok behaves differently too. Your nickname accepts styled text, your @username refuses it outright, and your caption counts it under a different rule again. Pick the wrong field and the letters disappear the second you press save. This page shows which style belongs in which field, and what each one costs you.

Your @username is the strict one. TikTok allows letters, numbers, underscores and periods, and nothing else. A period cannot sit at the end of it. You get one change every thirty days, that change rewrites your profile link, and a verified account can lose its badge in the process.

Fair warning: a lot of font sites tell you that styled letters work in the @username. They do not. Paste one in and TikTok either strips it back to plain or refuses the change outright. The allowed characters are listed on TikTok's own support page and that list has not moved.

The nickname is the field you actually want. TikTok calls it your nickname rather than your display name, which is why searching for display name rules turns up so little. It holds thirty characters, accepts Unicode, and appears in the feed, in comments and in messages. TikTok's page lists no waiting period between nickname changes.

That reach is what makes the nickname worth styling. It sits beside every comment you leave, at the top of every message thread, and under your video in the feed, so one change is doing work in several places at once. Your bio only appears when somebody opens your profile. If you are going to style one field and leave the rest plain, the nickname gives you more for the same effort.

<h2 id="styles">Every TikTok Font Style Worth Using</h2>

Twelve styles are worth your time on TikTok. They split into three groups by how much space they take up. The cheap group is the one most guides skip past, and it is the group that matters most inside a 160-character bio.

### Cheap: The Full 160 Characters

Four styles cost you nothing extra. Small Caps, Superscript, Fullwidth and Circled letters all sit in the older part of Unicode, which means each one takes a single slot. You can fill all 160 characters of your bio with any of them and still have the same room you started with.

Small Caps letters give you ᴄʜɪʟʟ ʜᴏᴜʀꜱ, which reads cleanly at small sizes and looks deliberate rather than decorated. Superscript shrinks the whole line to ᶜʰⁱˡˡ ʰᵒᵘʳˢ, which suits a short tagline better than a full sentence. Our small text generator keeps every one of those tiny alphabets in one place.

Fullwidth spreads your letters out as Ｃｈｉｌｌ Ｈｏｕｒｓ, with a wide gap built into every character. It costs the same as plain text but looks twice as long, which is a useful trade in a short bio. Our aesthetic font generator lines up the wide and boxy sets side by side.

### Mid-cost: Around One Hundred Characters

Classic Script and blackletter sit in the middle of the range. Both measure roughly twenty-two units for a fourteen-character phrase, which leaves you about one hundred characters of usable bio. That is enough for a short styled line with a few plain words after it.

Classic Script gives you 𝒞𝒽𝒾𝓁𝓁 ℋℴ𝓊𝓇𝓈, with the loops of handwriting. Bold Script pushes the same shapes harder as 𝓒𝓱𝓲𝓵𝓵 𝓗𝓸𝓾𝓻𝓼 and costs slightly more per letter. Our cursive font generator traces where those looping letters come from.

Fraktur reads as ℭ𝔥𝔦𝔩𝔩 ℌ𝔬𝔲𝔯𝔰, heavy and old. It reads slowly, so it does better across a short nickname than a full bio. Both of these styles share a habit that catches people out, and the examples table below shows it plainly.

### Expensive: Around Eighty-Six Characters

Bold, italic, monospace and double-struck are the heaviest four. A fourteen-character phrase measures twenty-four to twenty-six units in these styles, which cuts your usable bio down to somewhere near eighty-six characters. They still work well in a thirty-character nickname, where the limit is generous relative to the amount of text.

Bold Sans prints 𝗖𝗵𝗶𝗹𝗹 𝗛𝗼𝘂𝗿𝘀 and stays the most readable of the styled options. Bold, 𝐂𝐡𝐢𝐥𝐥 𝐇𝐨𝐮𝐫𝐬, carries more weight in the strokes. Handwriting leans the line over as 𝘊𝘩𝘪𝘭𝘭 𝘏𝘰𝘶𝘳𝘴. Our bold text generator weighs up bold against italic properly.

Monospace lines every letter up on an even grid as 𝙲𝚑𝚒𝚕𝚕 𝙷𝚘𝚞𝚛𝚜. Double-struck hollows the strokes out into ℂ𝕙𝕚𝕝𝕝 ℍ𝕠𝕦𝕣𝕤, which reads as technical rather than pretty. Both sit near the top of the cost range, so keep them for your nickname rather than your bio.

<h2 id="cost">Why Some Styles Cost You Half Your Bio</h2>

This is the part that should change how you pick a style. Every character on your screen is stored as one or more units behind the scenes, and most platforms count the units rather than the characters. A letter that looks like a single letter can be charged as two.

Unicode is split into sections. The oldest and busiest section holds ordinary letters, small caps, superscript, fullwidth characters and circled letters, and everything in there costs a single unit. The styled alphabets added later, the ones that give you bold, script, blackletter and monospace, live much further out. Anything from that outer area is stored as two units instead of one.

That is why the same fourteen-character phrase measured fourteen in Small Caps and twenty-six in Bold Sans. Nothing changed on screen. The storage behind it doubled.

Here is what that looks like on a real bio. A line reading chill hours, new video every friday is thirty-five characters as plain text. In Small Caps it still measures thirty-five, leaving you one hundred and twenty-five characters spare. In Bold Sans the same words measure around sixty-five, leaving you ninety-five. Same words, same screen, very different amount of room left over.

Mixing styles is the usual way out of that squeeze. Put the expensive style on the first two or three words where it does the most work, then let the rest of the line run as plain text. A bio opening with three Bold Sans words and finishing plain costs you about six extra characters rather than thirty. The eye reads the styled part as a heading and the plain part as the detail, which is easier to follow anyway.

Now for the part that surprised us. Classic Script and blackletter came in at twenty-two rather than twenty-six, which makes them cheaper than bold. That gap is not a rounding error, and the reason behind it is genuinely odd.

A handful of letters in those alphabets were never given a new home. Characters like ℭ, ℌ, ℋ and ℴ already existed in an older maths section from decades earlier, so Unicode reused them instead of drawing duplicates. Those reused letters sit in the cheap part of the range, which means every time your text lands on one, you pay half price for it. Our gothic font generator explains why five of its capitals look borrowed.

The practical version of all this is short. If your text is long and your field is tight, pick from the cheap group. If your text is short, like a thirty-character nickname, the expensive styles will fit comfortably and you can choose on looks alone.

<h2 id="stripped">What TikTok Deletes the Moment You Hit Save</h2>

TikTok filters certain characters on the way to saving your profile. The letters look fine in the edit box, you press save, and they come back plain or missing altogether. Knowing the list in advance saves a lot of retyping.

Combining marks are the clearest case. These are the accent-style marks that stack on top of letters, and TikTok removes them from bios during the save. Our glitch text generator pushes the same idea until it breaks, and that is exactly the kind of text TikTok will not keep. Use it where it is allowed and leave your TikTok bio out of it.

Zero-width characters are half blocked. The two most common invisible characters, U+200B and U+200C, get filtered out, while the Hangul filler at U+3164 and the blank braille character at U+2800 still pass through. People use those to make a nickname look empty, and TikTok has been closing the gap steadily.

Flipped text is a different case. It survives the save because it uses ordinary standalone letters rather than stacked marks. Our upside down text generator reads back to front on purpose, which is entertaining in a caption and awkward in a bio that people need to actually read.

Two more behaviours are worth listing here. If TikTok refuses a nickname outright you will see the message this nickname is not allowed, which usually points at a single character rather than your choice of words. And if your styled name vanishes after an app update, TikTok has reset it to your account default rather than deleting anything.

Heads-up: changing your display name more than about ten times in one day starts getting blocked. There is no warning before it happens. Test your styles in a notes app first, then make one change once you have settled on something.

<h2 id="tiktok-sans">The Font TikTok Uses Is Not Proxima Nova Any More</h2>

Ask what font TikTok uses and most answers will tell you Proxima Nova Semibold. That answer was correct for years. It stopped being correct in 2023 and the correction has not spread far.

TikTok now uses a typeface of its own, called TikTok Sans. The original was drawn in 2023 by Grilli Type, a Swiss foundry, as a brand face for the company. A brand face and a working app typeface are not the same job, though, so a second round of work followed and ran for roughly nine months.

That stage was handled by Type Network alongside Contrast Foundry, with an engineer brought in for the technical side. What came out is a variable font carrying seven weights and three adjustable axes for weight, slant and width. It reaches past four hundred and sixty languages across Latin, Greek and Cyrillic. For a company shipping one app to most of the world, that coverage is the entire point of the exercise.

The confirmation comes from TikTok itself. Their developer blog states that TikTok Sans is available in the app as Classic, Bold and Oblique among other options. So the font labelled Classic in your video editor is TikTok Sans, and has been for a while.

In 2025 TikTok released the whole family under an open licence and put it on Google Fonts. You can download it free, use it commercially, and install it on your own machine. Good to know: none of that helps you style your bio. A downloaded font lives on your computer, while your bio needs characters that travel along with the text, which is what the generator above produces.

The fonts inside the video editor work the same way. Classic, Typewriter, Handwriting, NEON and Serif are drawn by the app directly onto your video. They cannot be copied out, because there is nothing there to copy. What you are looking at is a picture of letters rather than letters themselves.

<h2 id="colours">What Yellow Font and Pink Font Really Mean</h2>

This question comes up constantly and it has almost nothing to do with everything above. Yellow font and pink font are caption colours in the TikTok video editor, not Unicode styles. You pick them from the colour swatch when you add text to a video.

Several font sites will tell you these colours are purely visual and carry no meaning at all. That is wrong. The colours turned into a signal, and viewers read them as one. The habit even has a name, font theory, and creators tag their own captions with it.

| Colour | What people read into it |
| --- | --- |
| Yellow | Something raw and personal. Oversharing, on purpose |
| Pink | Something romantic or soft. Crushes, relationships, longing |

Captions tagged yellow font btw are the clearest sign of the pattern. The writer is flagging that the words underneath are more honest than the video looks. Mainstream press has covered the trend at length, so it is well past being an inside joke among a few accounts.

Quick note: a second reading exists for bright yellow specifically, tied to business and self-improvement videos rather than emotion. Both readings are in circulation right now. Which one your audience reaches for depends largely on what they already watch.

<h2 id="readable">What a Screen Reader Hears When You Style Your Nickname</h2>

Styled Unicode letters are not letters as far as software is concerned. They are separate characters that happen to look like letters, and screen reading software treats them accordingly. The result is not what most people expect.

The word hello written as 𝐡𝐞𝐥𝐥𝐨 does not get read as hello. A screen reader announces it one character at a time, using each character's full technical name, in a row. That behaviour is the default on VoiceOver, NVDA, TalkBack and ChromeOS. A request to change it has been sitting open on one of those projects since 2020 without a fix.

Search is a related problem. A styled nickname is a run of unusual characters rather than a word, so anything reading your profile as text may fail to match it. We have not checked whether TikTok search finds a styled nickname. A display name can only be changed once in seven days on the account used for these tests, so that check has not been run.

Old devices cause a separate problem. When a phone holds no drawn shape for a character, it prints an empty rectangle instead, and that rectangle has a name among type people: tofu. It is not a broken character or a failed paste, because the letter arrived exactly as you sent it. The device just has nothing to show for it, which is why the same bio can look perfect on a new handset and half empty on a five-year-old one.

None of this means avoid styled text. It means put it where the cost is lowest. Style your nickname if you like how it looks, and keep your bio readable, since that is the part carrying your actual information.
