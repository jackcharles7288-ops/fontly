<h2 id="how-to">How to Flip Your Text in Four Steps</h2>

### 1. Type or paste your text

Drop your words into the box at the top of this page. The output updates as you type, so you can watch a name or a bio line change character by character. Nothing is uploaded anywhere; the swap happens inside your browser.

### 2. Choose flipped, or flipped and reversed

These two settings do different jobs and the difference matters more than it looks. Flipped swaps each character for one that resembles its turned version but keeps the reading order, so Jam Doughnut becomes <code>ſɐɯ ◖onƃɥunʇ</code>. Flipped and reversed also runs the whole line backwards, giving <code>ʇnuɥƃno◖ ɯɐſ</code>, which is what you want if you are trying to look like the text has been rotated on the page.

Most people want the second one. The first is useful when you need each word to stay in place, such as a list or a set of tags.

### 3. Copy the result

Use the copy button rather than selecting the text by hand. Several of these characters sit outside the usual Latin range, and a manual drag can clip the last one without you noticing.

### 4. Paste it somewhere and look at it before you post

This is the step every other flipped-text page leaves out, and it is the one that saves you. Paste your line into the app you actually plan to use, on the device you actually plan to use, and read it back. Capital letters and digits are where this breaks, and you would rather find that out now than after two hundred people have seen a row of empty rectangles in your bio.

<h2 id="types">Flipped, Backwards and Mirrored Are Three Different Things</h2>

Three words get used as if they mean the same thing, and they do not. Unicode itself is precise about this, and the precision is built into the names of the characters. Once you know which word means what, half the tools online start making more sense.

| Unicode's word | What was done to the letter | Example | What people call it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <b>Turned</b> | Rotated 180 degrees | <code>ɐ</code> TURNED A | Upside down, flipped |
| <b>Reversed</b> | Reflected across a vertical line | <code>ǝ</code> TURNED E's cousin <code>Ǝ</code> REVERSED E | Mirrored |
| <b>Inverted</b> | Reflected across a horizontal line | very few exist | rarely used by anyone |
| <b>Sideways</b> | Rotated 90 degrees | a handful of vowels | almost never supported |

Unicode does not contain a complete upside-down alphabet. This tool therefore mixes genuinely turned letters with reversed letters, mathematical operators, characters borrowed from other writing systems and unchanged characters that already have a useful shape. The official Unicode name in each row shows what each substitute actually is.

Worth knowing: the phrase inverted text generator gets searched constantly, and in Unicode's own vocabulary it means the wrong thing. Inverted is a vertical flip, not a rotation. Nearly everyone typing that phrase wants turned characters, so tools quietly treat the two words as synonyms and nobody corrects it.

Mirrored text is a genuinely separate set. It uses reversed characters, and it reads correctly when you hold it up to an actual mirror. Here is the blunt version: most sites that advertise a mirror tool do not mirror anything.

They reverse the order of your letters and leave the letters themselves facing the right way, which produces backwards word order rather than a reflection. If a tool turns <code>hello</code> into <code>olleh</code>, that is reversed order. If it turns it into shapes that lean the other way, that is mirroring. Small text and subscript are a fourth thing again, built from a completely different set of blocks, and our [small text generator](/small-text-generator/) covers that set properly.

The rest is a tour through unrelated writing systems. <code>Ԁ</code> is Cyrillic, <code>Ό</code> is Greek, and <code>ᗺ</code> is Canadian syllabics, officially CANADIAN SYLLABICS CARRIER KHA.

<code>Ⅎ</code> traces back to a letter the Roman emperor Claudius tried to add to Latin. <code>ſ</code> is the long s, the character that makes eighteenth-century printing look like it says <i>houfe</i> instead of <i>house</i>. The live map draws from fourteen Unicode blocks, including Latin, phonetic, mathematical, geometric and syllabic ranges.

Three capitals sit in an awkward middle. <code>ᴎ</code>, <code>ᴚ</code> and <code>ᴧ</code> are small capitals, so they render perfectly well but come out shorter than the capitals around them. Nothing is broken, the line just looks slightly uneven.

The Lisu block, historically associated with the Fraser alphabet, was added in Unicode 5.2 in October 2009. This tool does not use Lisu characters, and Unicode still does not provide a complete upside-down capital alphabet.

<h2 id="numbers">Why Numbers Barely Work</h2>

Digits are the weakest part of flipped text by a wide margin, and no other page will tell you so. The table below is the honest version. It includes the two digits that have no flipped form at all.

| Digit | What you get | Is there a genuine turned character? | What is going on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | <code>0</code> | Not needed | Symmetrical, reads the same either way |
| 1 | <code>1</code> | <b>None exists</b> | Nothing in Unicode resembles a turned one |
| 2 | <code>ᘔ</code> | Previous mapping only, U+218A TURNED DIGIT TWO | Live output is <code>ᘔ</code>, U+1614 CANADIAN SYLLABICS CARRIER JU, a borrowed substitute |
| 3 | <code>Ɛ</code> | Previous mapping only, U+218B TURNED DIGIT THREE | Live output is <code>Ɛ</code>, U+0190 LATIN CAPITAL LETTER OPEN E, a borrowed substitute |
| 4 | <code>ᔭ</code> | <b>None exists</b> | <code>ᔭ</code> is U+152D CANADIAN SYLLABICS YA, a borrowed visual substitute |
| 5 | <code>5</code> | <b>None exists</b> | Nothing in Unicode resembles a turned five |
| 6 | <code>9</code> | Pairs with 9 | The two digits already flip into each other |
| 7 | <code>Ɫ</code> | No | An L with a middle tilde, borrowed |
| 8 | <code>8</code> | Not needed | Symmetrical |
| 9 | <code>6</code> | Pairs with 6 | The pair again, running the other way |

Two digits have nothing at all. Zero and eight are symmetrical so they need no partner, six and nine already turn into each other, and the rest are patched with borrowed characters. That leaves a set which works properly for about half of what you type.

The two and the three are the interesting case. Genuine turned versions of both do exist, <code>↊</code> and <code>↋</code>, and they sit in the Number Forms block alongside the Roman numerals. They were the previous mapping, added in Unicode 8.0 in 2015 for duodecimal arithmetic, a base-twelve counting system, where they stand in for ten and eleven. Bio decoration was never the point. The live tool no longer emits them, and what you get now is <code>ᘔ</code> for two and <code>Ɛ</code> for three, borrowed visual substitutes that another phone may still fail to draw.

There is a neat reason the one and the five were never solved. On a seven-segment display, the kind in a pocket calculator, an upside-down two is indistinguishable from a five. The shapes collide, so no separate character was ever worth designing.

Quick note on the four: the person who wrote the character mapping that almost every generator on the web copied left a comment admitting it was the weakest entry in the table. That admission has been sitting in public for years while the rest of the internet reused the map anyway. Nobody has fixed it since. No encoded turned digit four exists, so the live four is <code>ᔭ</code>, U+152D CANADIAN SYLLABICS YA, a borrowed visual substitute.

So Jam Doughnut 45 comes out as <code>5ᔭ ʇnuɥƃno◖ ɯɐſ</code>. The four turns into a syllabics character that only half resembles it, and the five stays stubbornly upright. There is no honest way around that. If you want digits that genuinely change shape, our [number font generator](/number-font-generator/) carries proper styled sets built for the job.

## Nothing Actually Flips. Here Is What Really Happens

No rotation happens anywhere in this process. Your phone does not turn anything over, and no image is generated at any point. What happens is a lookup. The tool holds a table with one row per character, reads your text one character at a time, and swaps each one for a different character that already existed in Unicode long before anyone thought of using it this way. Then, if you picked the reversed setting, it reads the whole line backwards.

The clearest proof sits in your own name. When your <code>n</code> appears to flip, you are not looking at a rotated <code>n</code>. You are looking at a <code>u</code>, an ordinary letter that has been in every font since fonts began, which happens to look like an upside-down <code>n</code>. The same trick runs the other way with <code>b</code> and <code>q</code>, and with <code>d</code> and <code>p</code>.

Everything else is the same trick with less convenient source material. There is no letter that resembles a rotated <code>a</code>, so the table reaches into the phonetic alphabet and borrows <code>ɐ</code>, a symbol linguists use for a specific vowel sound. There is nothing that resembles a rotated capital A, so it reaches into mathematics and takes <code>∀</code>.

This is why the effect behaves so unevenly. A font that ships with the phonetic block will render your lowercase perfectly and still fail on a maths operator, because those characters live in different parts of the standard and get supported at different times by different devices. It is also why flipped text can be copied, pasted, saved and searched as ordinary text while still looking like a picture of upside down writing. Every one of those symbols is a real, individually numbered character with a name and a purpose. The purpose just has nothing to do with being upside down.

<h2 id="compatibility">Why Some Flipped Letters Show as Boxes, and Which Ones Never Do</h2>

Typographers call it tofu, because a hollow box looks like a block of it, and Google named its Noto font family after the goal of having no tofu left anywhere. Tofu appears when your device has the character but the font in front of you has no drawing for it.

The usual explanation gets this backwards. Your text arrived intact and the character is present; the font in front of you just has no picture to draw, so the system puts a placeholder box there instead.

## What Machines Do With Your Flipped Text

People can read flipped text with a bit of effort. Software has a harder time, and it fails in ways that matter. Three of those failures show up often enough to plan around.

### Search stops finding you

Platform search engines match the characters you typed, not the characters they resemble. A flipped name is stored as a string of phonetic and mathematical symbols, so a person typing your actual name will not match it. This applies to Instagram search, Facebook search, Discord member lists and the search box inside almost every app you use.

Good to know: this cuts both ways. If you want to be findable, keep your searchable fields in plain characters. If you want a caption to look unusual and you do not care about being found through it, flipped text costs you nothing.

### Screen readers read it wrong, not silently

Screen readers encounter the actual Unicode characters, not the shapes they resemble. Because this map mixes phonetic letters, mathematical operators and characters from other scripts, pronunciation can vary by reader, browser, language and settings. VoiceOver, TalkBack, NVDA and JAWS were not tested in this round, so do not use flipped text for essential information.

### It is not encryption, and one site should stop saying so

You will find pages claiming flipped text provides basic data encryption and suggesting it for sensitive information. That claim is false and worth stating plainly. Here is why it does not hold up.

Flipped text is not encryption. This tool does not reliably decode its own output when you paste it back, because many substitutions are one-way. The public mapping is still predictable and offers no protection for sensitive information.

## If You Are in Word, Canva or Your Own CSS, Do Not Use These

Here is the section most generator pages bury at the bottom, if they include it at all. When you are working inside software that has real formatting, that software will do this better than we can. Rotating an actual text box gives you all 26 letters, every digit, correct capitals, no boxes on anyone's device, and text that remains fully searchable and readable by assistive software. Character substitution exists for the places that forbid formatting, such as a bio field or a chat message, and that is the whole of its job.

- <b>Microsoft Word:</b> To flip or rotate text in Word, insert a Text Box or WordArt, then use Shape Format and Rotate. For a true mirror, open Format Shape, go to Text Options, then Text Effects, then 3-D Rotation, and set X Rotation to 180 degrees.
- <b>Excel:</b> Use the Orientation control on the Home tab to rotate cell contents.
- <b>Canva:</b> Select the text box and drag the rotation handle, or flip it from the Position panel.
- <b>Your own website:</b> <code>transform: rotate(180deg);</code> in CSS rotates the element and leaves the underlying text intact for search engines and screen readers.

Reality check: if you are designing a poster, a document or a web page, that last line is the correct answer and this page's tool is the wrong one. Use the tool above for the boxes where you have no other option.

## Upside Down Question Marks and Exclamation Marks Are Not Upside Down Text

A lot of people arrive here wanting the upside down question mark <code>¿</code> or the reverse exclamation mark <code>¡</code>, and those two are a separate subject with a separate answer.

They are Spanish punctuation. Spanish needs them because a question and a statement can use identical word order, so without an opening mark you do not know a sentence is a question until you reach the end of it. Compare <i>Te gusta el verano</i> with <i>¿Te gusta el verano?</i> The Real Academia Española recommended the inverted marks in 1754, in the second edition of its orthography, and they have been standard ever since. Not every language that borrowed Spanish conventions agreed; Catalan's language authority instructs writers never to use them, and Pablo Neruda refused them outright.

You do not need a generator for these. Your keyboard already has them.

| Where you are | <code>¿</code> | <code>¡</code> |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Windows, any app | Alt + 0191 | Alt + 0161 |
| Microsoft Word | type <code>00BF</code> then Alt + X | type <code>00A1</code> then Alt + X |
| Mac | Shift + Option + ? | Option + 1 |
| iPhone or Android | hold the <code>?</code> or <code>!</code> key | hold the <code>!</code> key |
| Spanish keyboard layout | the key to the right of <code>0</code> | same key with Shift |

Now the part that connects this back to the rest of the page. Both characters do appear in the live mapping, and they are the only two entries in the entire set that belong there by design. Somebody sat down and drew an upside-down question mark on purpose, because a language needed one. Every other character in that chart is a coincidence, a letter from Canadian syllabics or Cyrillic or the phonetic alphabet that happens to look right when you squint. Two deliberate marks, and two dozen accidents.
